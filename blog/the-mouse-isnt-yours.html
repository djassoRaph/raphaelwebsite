The mouse isn't yours

September 23, 2026 ~3 min read Hardware Right to Repair Bluetooth

Back in June, I lost a mouse dongle. Not the computer mouse; the mouse is fine. Just the little USB receiver that pairs it. Two hours later I was googling "universal dongle" to solve a problem HP could have closed in thirty seconds.

Here is the chain. HP's AI support could not help, so it handed me to commercial. Commercial handed me to one of their wholesalers in Paris. The wholesaler handed me to an individual retailer. The retailer told me they do not do that part. So I ended up on Google, searching for a "universal dongle": third-party receivers that claim to pair with any wireless mouse. Maybe one would work with mine. I was not sure, and I was even less sure of the pairing procedure if it did.

Nobody in that chain was built to help. Each link existed to pass the buck; to throw the ball somewhere else and confirm, politely, that the answer was no. That is not support. It is a maze with a customer-shaped exit at the far end, and the exit opens onto a Google search.

And that is the pattern with corporate support in general: the quality is low because support was never the point. The whole machine is tuned to sell you the next product, not to make the current one good enough to keep. A company that cared about its products would treat a lost receiver as a thirty-second sale and a customer kept; instead it is a problem to route away until you give up and buy a new mouse.

So is it really yours?

It reminds me of Clippy. Not the paperclip itself; the instinct behind it. A company so sure it knows what you need that it never checks whether the thing still works. HP sells the mouse; it will not sell the receiver that makes the mouse a mouse. So is it really yours? You bought it; you own the plastic. But the moment one proprietary part goes missing, the manufacturer shrugs and the device becomes landfill, unless a factory in Shenzhen decides to care. I made the same argument about software in Whose computer is this, actually?; this is the hardware version.

Which is the real question: what is HP's goal here? Computers? Printers? Mice? It is hard to tell, because none of those lines seems to come with the boring follow-through that ownership implies; spare parts, replacements, a path back to working. A company that sells hardware but will not sell the parts to keep it alive is not in the hardware business. It is in the replacement business. On this evidence, I am not sure HP should be in the peripherals market at all.

The technical lesson

The technical lesson is simpler than the rant. That mouse uses a proprietary pairing protocol; lose the matched dongle and the device is dead until you track down that exact receiver. It turned into a paper weight for a couple of months. Logitech's Unifying receivers carry the same trap. A Bluetooth mouse does not; pair it to anything, forever, with no dongle to lose. So when you spec peripherals, for yourself or for a team, the choice is not about brand. It is about whether you want to own the device or rent it from whoever still stocks the dongle.

PS: I found the dongle again. xD
